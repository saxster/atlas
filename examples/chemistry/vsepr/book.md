Every glass of water you have ever drunk is made of trillions upon trillions of tiny bent things. Each water molecule is an oxygen atom with two hydrogen atoms attached, and the two hydrogens do not sit on opposite sides of the oxygen. They stick out at an angle, like the two arms of a boomerang, about 104.5° apart.

That bend matters more than it sounds. It is part of the reason water clings to glass, soaks into cloth and dissolves the salt you stir into dal. This chapter is about where the bend comes from, and the surprising thing is that you can work it out by counting.

## 1.1 Pairs that push

:::scene Oxford, 1940

In 1940, with Britain at war, two chemists from Oxford gave one of the Royal Society's grand lectures, the Bakerian Lecture. Nevil Sidgwick was in his late sixties and had spent almost his whole working life at Lincoln College, Oxford, thinking about how atoms join together. Herbert Powell was in his early thirties and an expert on crystals, and on using X-rays to find where every atom in a crystal sits. Between them they had collected the shapes of a great many molecules, and they had noticed a pattern.

The pattern was this. Look at the central atom of a molecule and count the pairs of electrons in its outer layer: the pairs it shares with its neighbours, and any pairs it keeps to itself. Two pairs, and the molecule is a straight line. Three pairs, a flat triangle. Four pairs, a little pyramid with a triangular base, called a tetrahedron. The number of pairs seemed to decide the shape. (A Japanese chemist, Ryutaro Tsuchida, had published a similar idea in Japan a year earlier, in 1939.)

Seventeen years later, in 1957, two chemists at University College London, Ronald Gillespie and his Australian colleague Ronald Nyholm, took the pattern and turned it into a set of rules that could choose between the shapes a molecule might have. They had been talking about how to help students understand why molecules have the shapes they do. Their rules became the idea in this chapter.

:::predict
A water molecule has an oxygen atom in the middle and a hydrogen atom on each side. What shape is it?
- A straight line: H–O–H, with the hydrogens 180° apart, as far from each other as they can be
- Bent, with the hydrogens at a right angle, 90° apart
- Bent, with the hydrogens a little more than 100° apart
???
The third. The angle between the two O–H bonds has been measured very precisely, using the way water molecules absorb microwaves and infrared light: it is 104.5°. To see why, you have to count something you cannot see in the formula H₂O: the oxygen's lone pairs.
:::

Here is the idea, in plain words. Electrons all carry the same negative charge, and like charges push each other away. Around the central atom of a molecule, the electrons come in pairs, and each pair is a cloud of negative charge. Every cloud pushes on every other cloud. The clouds end up where those pushes balance, and that is as far apart as they can get while all being held to the same nucleus.

Some pairs are shared between the central atom and a neighbour: those are the bonds. Others belong to the central atom alone: chemists call them lone pairs. Bond or lone pair, each one is a region of negative charge, and each one counts.

Now count for methane, the main gas in the piped cooking gas that many Mumbai kitchens use. Carbon has four electrons in its outer layer, and each of four hydrogen atoms brings one more. That makes four shared pairs, four bonds, and nothing else. Four clouds, pushing each other apart round a ball.

How do you put four things round a ball so they are as far apart as possible? Your first guess might be a flat cross, like a plus sign, with the pairs 90° apart. But a ball has an up and a down as well as a left and right. Lift two of the arms up and push the other two down, and every pair gets further from every other. The best you can do is a tetrahedron. Stand it on a table and it looks like a tripod with a fourth leg pointing straight up. Every angle between two arms is the same, 109.5°. (It is exactly the angle whose cosine is −1/3, which you can prove with the corners of a cube, as the companion figure shows.)

:::home Balloon molecules
You need four long party balloons, the kind sold for decorations at a birthday or a Ganpati pandal, and some thread.

1. Blow up two balloons to the same size and tie them together at their knots. Let go. They swing round until they point in opposite directions: a straight line.
2. Tie a third balloon on at the same knot. The three spread out into a flat triangle, a third of a turn apart.
3. Tie on a fourth. The four will not lie flat. They twist into a tripod with one balloon pointing straight up: a tetrahedron. Set it on the table and look at it from above, then from the side.
4. Now swap one balloon for a rounder, fatter one. Watch the other three: they are squeezed a little closer together.

The balloons are an analogy, not the real thing. Balloons push by touching: each one takes up room, and the others are shoved aside. Electron pairs push from a distance, through the electric force, and they have no skins. But the result is the same, for a similar reason: each thing pushes the others away until the pushes balance, and the only arrangement where they balance is the one where they are spread out evenly.
:::

## 1.2 The lone pairs squeeze

Now ammonia, the sharp-smelling gas in some cleaning liquids. Its central atom is nitrogen, which has five electrons in its outer layer. Three of them pair up with three hydrogen atoms, making three bonds. The other two stay on the nitrogen as one lone pair. Three bonds plus one lone pair: four pairs again, so they still spread out roughly into a tetrahedron.

But not exactly. The angle between two N–H bonds is not 109.5°. It is 106.7°, a little narrower. Something is pushing the bonds together, and the something is the lone pair.

A bond pair is held between two nuclei, the nitrogen's and a hydrogen's. Both pull on it, so it is stretched out along the line between them, like a sausage. A lone pair is held by the nitrogen alone. Nothing pulls it outwards, so it stays close to the nitrogen and spreads out sideways, fat and short. Close to the nitrogen, where all four pairs crowd together, the lone pair takes up more room than a bond. It is the fat balloon in your experiment, and it pushes the three bonds closer together.

Water has one more lone pair. Oxygen has six electrons in its outer layer: two go into bonds with the hydrogens, and the other four make two lone pairs. Two bonds and two lone pairs: four pairs, roughly a tetrahedron, with the two fat lone pairs pointing up and away, and the two bonds pushed down and together. The angle closes further, to 104.5°.

So you get a neat ladder. Methane, no lone pairs: 109.5°. Ammonia, one: 106.7°. Water, two: 104.5°. Each lone pair takes a couple of degrees off.

Notice something about the names. Chemists name a molecule's shape by where its atoms are, because the atoms are what experiments find. Methane's atoms make a tetrahedron. Ammonia's four atoms make a low pyramid, with the nitrogen on top: chemists say "trigonal pyramidal". Water's three atoms make a bent line, and chemists just say "bent". The lone pairs are not in the name, but they are the reason for the shape.

:::careful
> "Water is H–O–H, so it must be a straight line, with the hydrogens on opposite sides of the oxygen."

The way we write a formula on paper says which atoms are joined, not where they sit in space. Count the pairs on the oxygen: two bonds and two lone pairs. Four pairs spread out round the oxygen, so the two bonds cannot be on opposite sides. They are 104.5° apart. A straight water molecule would have no positive end and no negative end, and it would be far worse at dissolving salt.
:::

:::careful
> "Lone pairs aren't part of the shape, so they don't matter."

They are not in the shape's name, because the name describes where the atoms are. But they are very much there, pushing. Take away ammonia's lone pair (which is what happens when ammonia picks up a hydrogen ion and becomes ammonium, NH₄⁺) and its four bonds spread out into a perfect tetrahedron at 109.5°.
:::

:::five VSEPR
? What is it?
A way of predicting the shape of a molecule by counting the electron pairs around its central atom. The pairs push each other apart, and they settle as far apart as they can; the atoms end up wherever the bond pairs point.
? Why is it called this?
VSEPR stands for Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion. The valence shell is an atom's outer layer of electrons, the ones that take part in bonding; electron pairs are the two-electron clouds in it; repulsion is the push between like charges. Chemists say it "vesper".
? What problem did it solve?
By 1940 chemists had measured the shapes of many molecules but had no simple way to say why each one had its shape. VSEPR lets you predict a shape with a pencil, before anyone measures it.
? Why does it work?
Electron pairs are all negatively charged, so they repel each other, and the arrangement where the pushes balance is the one with the pairs spread out evenly. Lone pairs are held by one nucleus only, so they sit closer to it and spread wider, and they push harder than bond pairs.
? How does it help?
It explains why methane is a tetrahedron, ammonia a pyramid and water bent, and it puts their angles in the right order. Bent water is part of why water dissolves so many things.
?? How do we know?
The angles are measured. Molecules absorb microwaves and infrared light at frequencies that depend on how their atoms are arranged, and from those frequencies the bond lengths and angles can be worked out: 109.5° for methane, 106.7° for ammonia, 104.5° for water.
?? What it isn't
It is not a calculation. VSEPR says water's angle is less than 109.5°, not that it is 104.5°. And electron pairs are not little balls on sticks: they are clouds, and the rule is a way of thinking about them, not a picture of them.
?? Where does it stop working?
Sulfur sits below oxygen in the periodic table, so hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, has the same count as water: two bonds, two lone pairs. VSEPR says "a bit less than 109.5°". The measured angle is 92.1°, nearly a right angle. Explaining that needs the full quantum theory of how electrons behave, not a counting rule.
:::

## 1.3 Where the rule runs out

A good rule of thumb is worth having, and VSEPR is a very good one. It gives the right shape for most of the small molecules you will meet, and it ranks their angles correctly. But it is honest to say what it cannot do.

It cannot give you the numbers. Nothing in the rule says that a lone pair takes about 2.5° off an angle rather than 1° or 5°. And for some molecules it gets even the rough size wrong. Hydrogen sulfide, the gas that gives rotten eggs their smell, has a sulfur atom with two bonds and two lone pairs, just like water. VSEPR expects something close to water's angle. Measure it and you find 92.1°. Phosphine, PH₃, has the same count as ammonia but an angle of 93.3° instead of 106.7°.

The bigger atoms are the trouble. Sulfur and phosphorus are larger than oxygen and nitrogen, their bonds are longer, and their bond pairs are not crowded together near the centre the way they are in water. The full explanation needs the quantum theory of electrons, which you will meet later. For now it is enough to know where the rule works, where it does not, and that the measurements always have the last word.

:::check
Carbon dioxide, CO₂, has a carbon atom joined to two oxygen atoms by double bonds, and no lone pairs on the carbon. A double bond counts as one region of electrons. What shape does VSEPR predict for CO₂, and what angle?
???
Two regions of electrons on the carbon (one for each double bond) and no lone pairs. Two regions spread out as far as possible point in opposite directions, so CO₂ is a straight line: O=C=O, with an angle of 180°. Compare water: it also has two atoms on the central atom, but its oxygen has two lone pairs as well, four regions in all, so water is bent.
:::
